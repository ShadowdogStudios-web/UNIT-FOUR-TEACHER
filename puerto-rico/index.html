Unit 4 · Deep Dive · Puerto Rico

The Island in Between

In 1898 Puerto Rico traded one empire’s flag for another’s. For the next twenty years, Puerto Ricans kept asking the same question: what are we now?

Driving question

What did the United States promise Puerto Rico, and what did it deliver?

Guánica harbor, where General Miles’s army first landed in Puerto Rico — stereograph by Strohmeyer & Wyman, about 1899 (one frame), public domain (Library of Congress)
1493–1898

Borinquen

The Taíno people called the island Borikén. Spain claimed it in 1493 and ruled it for four centuries. By the 1890s, Puerto Ricans had built towns, railroads, coffee farms, and sugar plantations, and many wanted to govern themselves.

In November 1897, Spain finally agreed. A Charter of Autonomy gave Puerto Rico its own elected parliament and cabinet. The new parliament met for the first time in July 1898. Eight days later, American troops landed.

July 25, 1898

The landing at Guánica

General Nelson Miles landed more than 3,000 American soldiers at Guánica, on the island’s south coast. His proclamation told Puerto Ricans that the Americans came to give them “the immunities and blessings of the liberal institutions of our Government.”

Many towns welcomed the troops. Some Puerto Ricans hoped the United States would bring more freedom than Spain had. The fighting lasted less than three weeks, until a cease-fire on August 12.

1898–1900

Soldiers in charge

In the Treaty of Paris, signed in December 1898, Spain handed Puerto Rico to the United States. Puerto Ricans were not asked. The U.S. Army ran the island, and the autonomous government won from Spain was set aside. Official papers even spelled the name “Porto Rico.”

The Army set the value of one Puerto Rican peso at 60 U.S. cents, and dollars replaced pesos. Many families felt their savings had shrunk overnight.

August 8, 1899

San Ciriaco

A year after the war, the strongest hurricane in memory crossed the island. About 3,400 people died, many of them in floods. A quarter of a million people were left without enough food or shelter.

The storm destroyed most of that year’s coffee crop, the island’s most valuable export. The Army handed out food, but many small coffee farms never recovered. In the years that followed, sugar took coffee’s place.

1900

The Foraker Act

In April 1900, Congress replaced Army rule with a civilian government. Puerto Ricans could elect a 35-member House of Delegates. But the president of the United States appointed the governor and an 11-member Executive Council, and no law could pass without the council.

Puerto Rico also got a Resident Commissioner in Washington, who could speak in Congress but not vote. Puerto Ricans became “citizens of Porto Rico,” not citizens of the United States. For a time, goods traded between the island and the mainland were also taxed.

Be the Historian

“The Ill-Fated Sister”

The source. Louis Dalrymple drew this cartoon for Puck magazine in April 1900, the month the Foraker Act became law. President McKinley drives a carriage marked “Free Trade.” Uncle Sam rides with Hawaii, which had been given free trade. Puerto Rico stands by the road with a basket of fruit.

Look closer. In December 1899, McKinley had told Congress that free trade with Puerto Rico was “our plain duty.” Find that paper in the cartoon. Where did the cartoonist put it? Notice, too, how the artist drew both women: cartoons of the time used racial stereotypes even when they defended a cause.

Your turn. The title calls this “unjust discrimination.” Is the cartoonist on Puerto Rico’s side? How can you tell?

1901

Foreign in a domestic sense

Could Congress treat Puerto Rico differently from a state? In 1901 the Supreme Court said yes. In a group of rulings called the Insular Cases, the Court decided that the Constitution does not fully apply in territories Congress has not made a full part of the United States.

One justice, Edward White, called Puerto Rico “foreign to the United States in a domestic sense.” In plain words: it belonged to the United States, but it was not part of it. Those rulings have never been overturned.

1900s

Sugar

After 1901, Puerto Rican sugar could enter the United States without a tax. American companies poured money into huge sugar mills called centrales, and sugar exports grew many times over.

Most cane cutters earned low wages and had work only during the harvest. The months between harvests were called el tiempo muerto, “the dead time,” when many families went hungry. The island grew richer on paper while many workers stayed poor.

Be the Historian

Who wrote the caption?

The source. This stereograph was sold in the United States around 1900. Its printed title reads: “America’s greatest gift to Porto Rico — the public school, Caguas.”

The context. The new government built many new schools, and more children went to school than under Spain. But for years, most classes had to be taught in English, a language almost none of the children spoke at home. Teachers and parents pushed back, and Spanish did not become the main language of public schools again until 1949.

Your turn. Who made this picture, and who was it sold to? Would a family in Caguas have written the same caption?

1900–1916

Luis Muñoz Rivera

Luis Muñoz Rivera had led the campaign that won autonomy from Spain in 1897. Under American rule he kept fighting for self-government, and he called the Foraker Act a step backward from what Spain had granted.

In 1910, Puerto Ricans elected him Resident Commissioner. In Washington he could speak but not vote. In 1916 he told Congress that Puerto Ricans wanted to govern themselves. He died that November, a few months before Congress acted.

1917–1918

Citizens, then soldiers

On March 2, 1917, President Wilson signed the Jones-Shafroth Act. Puerto Ricans became citizens of the United States and could now elect both houses of their legislature, though the president still chose the governor. Anyone who refused U.S. citizenship had to say so in writing; 288 people did.

Two months later, Congress passed the draft. About 236,000 Puerto Rican men registered, and about 18,000 served. The island’s regiment guarded the Panama Canal. Puerto Ricans of African descent were placed in the segregated 375th Infantry Regiment. One of its officers, Pedro Albizu Campos, later led Puerto Rico’s independence movement. The composer Rafael Hernández joined the 369th “Hellfighters” band; hear his 1917 danzón “Patria” in the Music Hall.

Today

Still asking

Puerto Ricans elected their own governor for the first time in 1948, and in 1952 the island became a commonwealth with its own constitution. Puerto Ricans on the island are U.S. citizens, but they cannot vote for president, and their Resident Commissioner still cannot vote in Congress.

Puerto Ricans still debate whether the island should become a state, become independent, or keep its current status. More than five million people of Puerto Rican heritage live on the mainland, including a large community in Chicago, where two 59-foot steel Puerto Rican flags arch over Division Street in Humboldt Park.
