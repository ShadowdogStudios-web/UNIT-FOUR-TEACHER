Unit 4 · Deep Dive · Lake County

Two Bases on the Bluff

Fort Sheridan and Great Lakes stand a few miles from Waukegan. One was built to watch Chicago. The other was built to send sailors to the world.

Driving question

Who were these two bases built to protect?

Sailors in training spell “VICTORY” at Great Lakes Naval Training Station — photograph, about 1918, public domain (National Archives, via Wikimedia Commons)
Lake County

Ten miles of shoreline

Two military bases sit on the bluffs above Lake Michigan in Lake County. Great Lakes, the Navy’s training station, is just south of Waukegan in North Chicago. Fort Sheridan, an Army post, was about ten miles farther south.

They were built 24 years apart, for very different reasons. Together they tie this county to almost everything in Unit 4: strikes, the war with Spain, the First World War, and the epidemic that followed.

1877–1887

A fort built because of strikes

You met the reason in Unit 3. In 1877, railroad workers across the country went on strike, and there was fighting in Chicago’s streets. In 1886, a bomb exploded at a workers’ rally in Haymarket Square.

Chicago’s richest businessmen belonged to the Commercial Club. They wanted soldiers close to the city. In 1887 the club bought 632 acres on the lake at Highwood and gave the land to the federal government. Soldiers arrived that November. A year later the post was named for General Philip Sheridan.

1889–1891

The tower and the parade ground

The Chicago architects Holabird & Roche designed the fort’s buildings in cream-colored brick. At the center they placed a tall water tower, with long barracks stretching out on both sides.

In front of it lay a wide parade ground where soldiers drilled. The tower still stands today. It is the landmark you can see from the train and from the lakefront.

Be the Historian

Who was the fort for?

The evidence. In July 1894, during the Pullman strike, President Cleveland ordered federal troops into Chicago. Soldiers from Fort Sheridan were among the first to arrive. It was the one time the fort’s troops were used against a strike.

More evidence. In 1891, after the massacre at Wounded Knee, the Army held Lakota prisoners at Fort Sheridan. In 1898, the fort became a gathering point for soldiers going to the war with Spain.

Your turn. Look at those three uses. Was Fort Sheridan built to protect the people of Chicago, to control them, or both? Which piece of evidence matters most to your answer?

1904–1911

The Navy comes to the prairie

After the war with Spain, the United States had a two-ocean empire and needed a much bigger navy. Many of the best recruits were coming from the farms and towns of the Midwest.

Chicago business leaders again raised money for land, this time on the lake just south of Waukegan. The first recruits arrived in the summer of 1911, and President William Howard Taft came to dedicate the station on October 28, 1911. It had 39 brick buildings.

1917–1918

A city overnight

In early 1917, Great Lakes held about 1,500 sailors. Then the United States entered the First World War. By the end of the war the station had 776 buildings on 1,200 acres, and about 45,000 sailors were in training there at once.

In all, about 125,000 sailors trained at Great Lakes during the war. It was the largest naval training station in the country.

1917–1918

Sousa’s band

John Philip Sousa was the most famous bandleader in America, the composer of “The Stars and Stripes Forever.” In 1917, at age 62, he joined the Navy as a lieutenant and came to Great Lakes to train musicians.

More than 1,500 sailor-musicians trained under him. His 300-member band toured the country and played at rallies where Americans were urged to buy Liberty Bonds to pay for the war.

1917

Learning a new kind of war

In 1917, Fort Sheridan became a school for Army officers. Thousands of young men, many of them college students and businessmen from the Midwest, spent three months there learning to lead soldiers.

The war in France was fought from trenches behind tangles of barbed wire. So the trainees dug trenches on the bluffs of Lake County and practiced stringing wire, weeks before they would see the real thing.

Be the Historian

Posed, or real?

The source. This picture of men drilling at Fort Sheridan was sold on a card to be viewed in 3-D at home. A company called Keystone made thousands of such cards during the war.

The question. A company chose what to photograph and what to sell. Families bought the cards to feel close to the war.

Your turn. What does this picture show well? What would it never show? If you wanted to know what training really felt like, what other source would you look for?

September 1918

The unwelcome visitor

In September 1918, a deadly influenza reached Great Lakes with sailors transferred from Boston. The first cases were reported on September 7. Within two weeks, about one sailor in ten was sick.

The station’s hospital overflowed. Navy nurses worked until they fell ill themselves. Nearly a thousand sailors died at Great Lakes that fall, far from any battlefield.

Waukegan

Names you may know

A teenage violinist from Waukegan named Benny Kubelsky joined the Navy in 1918 and trained at Great Lakes. Playing for the sailors, he found that his jokes got a bigger reaction than his violin. He became the comedian Jack Benny.

George Halas also trained at Great Lakes and played on its football team, which won the Rose Bowl on New Year’s Day, 1919. He went on to found the Chicago Bears.

Today

Still on the bluff

Great Lakes is still open. Every new sailor in the United States Navy now goes through boot camp there.

Fort Sheridan closed as an Army post in 1993. Its old buildings became homes, and its bluffs and beach became a Lake County forest preserve. The tower still stands.
