Unit 4 · Sound & Screen · 1898–1918

War on Film

The war with Spain was the first American war people could watch at the movies. Edison’s camera crews filmed troops in Tampa and Cuba in 1898, and audiences lined up to see them. Twenty years later, the government had its own film crews in France. These films have no sound: they were shown with live music. Pick a record from the Music Hall to play along, or watch in silence.

1898–1899: The First War on Film

Edison’s cameras in Tampa, Cuba, and San Francisco, and the battles he staged in New Jersey.

Filmed in Havana harbor, 1898 · 0:57

Wreck of the Battleship “Maine”

Before you reveal. Real footage, or staged for the camera?

Reveal the answerReal footage

Filmed from a moving boat, weeks after the explosion. The twisted wreck sits in the harbor, surrounded by salvage boats.

Think about it. This was filmed after the explosion. What can this film prove about who sank the Maine? What can’t it prove?

Wreck of the Battleship “Maine” — motion picture, Thomas A. Edison, Inc., filmed in Havana harbor, 1898, public domain (Library of Congress) · source

Filmed at Key West, Florida, March 27, 1898 · 2:24

Burial of the “Maine” Victims

Sailors, Marines, and nine hearses, each coffin draped with the flag, pass through Key West.

Think about it. Edison sold this film to theaters a few weeks before the war began. How might audiences have felt watching it?

Burial of the “Maine” Victims — motion picture, Thomas A. Edison, Inc., filmed at Key West, Florida, March 27, 1898, public domain (Library of Congress) · source

Filmed at Port Tampa, Florida, 1898 · 1:01

Colored Troops Disembarking

Black soldiers of a regular Army infantry battalion climb down a steep gangplank. Edison’s catalog called the scene “laughable.”

Think about it. Edison’s catalog said it was “laughable to see” these soldiers. What does that description tell you about the audience Edison was selling to?

Colored Troops Disembarking — motion picture, Thomas A. Edison, Inc., filmed at Port Tampa, Florida, 1898, public domain (Library of Congress) · source

Filmed at Tampa, Florida, June 1898 · 0:45

Roosevelt’s Rough Riders Embarking for Santiago

Rough Riders load boxes onto a ship headed for Cuba.

Think about it. Roosevelt became a national hero from this war. How could films like this help a person become famous?

Roosevelt’s Rough Riders Embarking for Santiago — motion picture, Thomas A. Edison, Inc., filmed at Tampa, Florida, June 1898, public domain (Library of Congress) · source

Filmed in Cuba, June 22, 1898 · 0:40

U.S. Troops Landing at Daiquirí, Cuba

The first American troops to land in Cuba, filmed from the end of the pier by an Edison cameraman.

Think about it. This is real footage from Cuba. What details make it look real?

Cuban Volunteers Embarking

Cuban volunteers, with rifles and bedrolls, board a ship to go fight in their own country’s war for independence.

Think about it. Cubans had been fighting Spain for years before Americans arrived. Why do you think films of Cuban soldiers are so rare?

Filmed in New Jersey, 1899 · 0:58

Advance of Kansas Volunteers at Caloocan

Before you reveal. Real footage, or staged for the camera?

Reveal the answerReenacted

Edison’s catalog called it “one of the best battle pictures ever made.” It was staged: the New Jersey National Guard reenacted a battle from the Philippine-American War in the Orange Mountains.

Think about it. Before you read the label below the film: real or reenacted? What clues give it away?

Capture of Trenches at Candaba

Before you reveal. Real footage, or staged for the camera?

Reveal the answerReenacted

Another staged battle. National Guardsmen played both the Americans and the Filipinos. An officer on horseback “falls” dramatically.

Think about it. The men playing Filipinos were Americans. How might this film have shaped what audiences believed about the war?

Filmed in New Jersey, 1899 · 1:09

U.S. Infantry Supported by Rough Riders at El Caney

Before you reveal. Real footage, or staged for the camera?

Reveal the answerReenacted

A year after the real battle in Cuba, Edison restaged it with National Guardsmen, complete with charging horsemen firing revolvers.

1912–1914: The Canal on Film

Army films of the Panama Canal being finished.

Filmed about 1912 · 11:18

President Taft Inspects the Panama Canal

President Taft sails to Panama on the battleship Arkansas and tours the digging, the dredges, the Pedro Miguel and Miraflores locks, Gatun Lake, and the spillway. The film also shows “native dwellings.”

Think about it. Who appears on camera, and who is almost never shown up close? What does that tell you about whose story the film tells?

President Taft Inspects the Panama Canal — motion picture, U.S. Army Signal Corps (National Archives), filmed about 1912, public domain (National Archives, 111-M-331, via Internet Archive) · source

Filmed about 1912–1914 · 1:48

Panama Canal Activities

Electric locomotives pull boats through the locks, and a dredge works in Culebra Cut.

Think about it. The canal used electric “mules” to pull ships. What does this tell you about the technology of the canal?

Panama Canal Activities — motion picture, U.S. Army Signal Corps (National Archives), filmed about 1912–1914, public domain (National Archives, 111-M-330, via Internet Archive) · source

1917–1918: The Great War on Film

Propaganda, Chaplin, a British battle film, and Army footage of two divisions with Illinois soldiers.

Animated, 1918 · 0:45

W.S.S. Thriftettes

A short cartoon telling Americans their War Savings Stamps would help lock the Kaiser in a circus cage.

Think about it. This is propaganda for children and families. What does it want you to do, and how does it make the enemy look?

W.S.S. Thriftettes — motion picture, Promotion for War Savings Stamps, animated, 1918, public domain (Library of Congress) · source

Filmed in New York, 1918 · 2:41

President Wilson Leads the Fourth Liberty Loan Parade

President Wilson arrives in New York to march at the front of a parade selling war bonds.

Think about it. Why would the president himself march in a parade to sell bonds?

President Wilson Leads the Fourth Liberty Loan Parade — motion picture, Newsreel (Library of Congress), filmed in New York, 1918, public domain (Library of Congress) · source

1918 · 10:53

The Bond

Chaplin made this short film at his own expense for the Liberty Loan Committee. It shows “bonds” of friendship and love, then the Liberty Bond, and ends with Chaplin knocking out the Kaiser.

Think about it. Chaplin was the most famous movie star in the world. Why would the government want him selling bonds?

The Bond — motion picture, Written, directed by, and starring Charlie Chaplin, 1918, public domain (Internet Archive) · source

Filmed 1918; edited by the Army in 1921 · 10:29

The 33rd Division in France

Illinois soldiers

The 33rd was the Illinois National Guard division, the same one as the soldiers in the trench photograph in Chapter 5. The film shows troops landing in France, King George V decorating Illinois soldiers, telephone and telegraph crews, engineers building roads, and General Pershing awarding medals.

Think about it. This was made by the Army to record the war. What does it show a lot of? What does it leave out?

The 33rd Division in France — motion picture, U.S. Army Signal Corps (National Archives), filmed 1918; edited by the Army in 1921, public domain (National Archives, 111-M-33, reel 2, via Internet Archive) · source

Filmed 1918; edited by the Army in 1921 · 11:12

The 93rd Division in France

Illinois soldiers

The 93rd was a division of Black regiments, including Chicago’s 370th, the old Eighth Illinois. This film follows the 369th, its sister regiment from New York: soldiers with dogs and machine guns, a regimental band, a French general decorating the regiment’s flag, and carrier pigeons released during a gas alarm.

Think about it. The 93rd fought under French command. What clues in the film show the soldiers living and fighting alongside the French?

The 93rd Division in France — motion picture, U.S. Army Signal Corps (National Archives), filmed 1918; edited by the Army in 1921, public domain (National Archives, 111-M-93, via Internet Archive) · source

Hard history. This film shows wounded and dead soldiers. Your teacher will tell you whether to watch it.

British film, 1916 · 6:30

The Battle of the Somme (excerpt: “The Morning of the Attack”)

Twenty million British tickets were sold to see this film in 1916. This excerpt shows July 1, 1916, the first day of the battle, when about 19,000 British soldiers were killed. Historians have shown that the most famous scene, men going “over the top” with one falling back, was staged behind the lines. Much of the rest is real, including wounded and dead soldiers.

Think about it. Like the 1898 battle films, part of this was staged. Does a staged scene make the whole film untrustworthy? Why or why not?

The Battle of the Somme (excerpt: “The Morning of the Attack”) — motion picture, Filmed by Geoffrey Malins and John McDowell for the British government, british film, 1916, public domain (Internet Archive) · source

1918 · 37:14

Shoulder Arms

Chaplin plays a clumsy recruit who ends up in a flooded trench, disguises himself as a tree, and captures the Kaiser. It came out in October 1918, a month before the war ended, and soldiers loved it.

Think about it. Can a comedy tell the truth about war? Find one thing about trench life the film gets right.

Shoulder Arms — motion picture, Written, directed by, and starring Charlie Chaplin, 1918, public domain (Internet Archive) · source
